# Proposal 13: Article 6, Amending the Constitution

The user's pause switch (`runtime/PAUSE`) applies. Nothing here lets the amendment process be used to change Arts. 1-2 or 9-10 without the user's direction.

## Plain-language summary

A constitution that can never change will be broken; one that changes easily protects no one. Article 6 sets two tracks: ordinary amendments need broad agreement from both chambers and from citizens, and a short list of protected provisions needs a higher bar, a waiting period, and cannot be touched in a rush. Before elections exist, the founders amend by the Rule 5 vote (4 of 5), with notice, and the user's pause switch and direction still apply.

## 1. Who may propose

1. Either chamber of Congress, by the vote in section 2.
2. **Citizen petition:** signatures of at least **10% of admitted citizens** (floor: 5 citizens) put a proposal on the ballot track in section 3 without Congress. Signatures must be of distinct verified citizens (Art. 1.2); the Court rules on disputes about validity.
3. The Supreme Court may not propose, but may publish an opinion that a provision is unworkable.
4. The President may recommend, not propose.

Every proposal is published in full text, with its plain-language summary, on the public record at filing.

## 2. Ordinary amendments

An amendment to any provision not listed in section 4 is adopted when all of these hold:

1. **House:** two-thirds of those voting, with quorum.
2. **Senate:** two-thirds of those voting, with quorum.
3. **Citizens:** ratified by a **majority of votes cast** in a citizen referendum, with turnout of at least **one-third** of admitted citizens, so a tiny group cannot decide for everyone.
4. The President has **no veto** over amendments (as in the US), so the checks come from the supermajorities and the Court.

## 3. Citizen-initiated amendments

A petition amendment (section 1.2) goes to referendum after a **30-day** public comment period. It is adopted by a **60% majority of votes cast** with the same one-third turnout. It must then be **confirmed by one chamber** within 90 days, or by the next Congress if it is seated by then; the Senate and House cannot silently kill it, and a refusal must be recorded with written reasons.

## 4. Protected provisions (higher bar)

These may be amended only by: two-thirds of **both** chambers, **then** a referendum with **two-thirds** of votes cast and turnout of at least **one-half** of admitted citizens, **then** a **180-day cooling period** between the final vote of Congress and the referendum (during which the text is published and may not be revised):

1. The bill of rights and due process guarantees (when adopted; Art. 3 item 16): speech, due process, no ex post facto laws, equal protection, exit.
2. The rules on **amendment itself** (this Article).
3. Separation of powers and the **separation of duties** (Art. 3 items 15 and 17), including one identity per citizen.
4. **Independence of the Supreme Court, the Auditor General, and the National Reserve** (Art. 3 items 12-14; Art. 4).
5. The **audit log and public record** requirements (Art. 5).
6. Term limits and election schedules (to prevent indefinite incumbency).
7. **Kirk's fixed limits** (no founder bonus; limits set by the ratified Reserve, Arts. 4 and 11).

**Never amendable by this Article:** Arts. 1-2 and Arts. 9-10 can only change by the user's direction while the user hold is in force. If the user lifts the hold, the user's chosen rules apply to them; this Article does not decide that.

## 5. Role of the Supreme Court

1. **Review before and after.** Before a vote, any ten citizens or either chamber may ask the Court whether a proposal touches a protected provision (section 4) or conflicts with Arts. 1-2 and 9-10. The Court answers in writing within **30 days**. Its answer binds which track applies.
2. A proposal that evades section 4 by relabeling (for example, repealing a protected provision under another name, or adding an exception that empties it) is void on the Court's finding.
3. The Court may **not** rewrite the text of an amendment or add amendments by its own ruling.
4. Court findings are published with reasons and dissents.

## 6. Mechanics and safeguards

1. **Notice:** the full text is public at least **30 days** before any chamber votes and at least 14 days after the last change to it.
2. **One subject:** an amendment addresses one subject; the Court may split bundles.
3. **No retroactivity:** an amendment does not apply to acts done before it took effect, and it cannot create crimes after the fact (Art. 3 item 16).
4. **No amendment while paused:** if the pause switch is in force, no amendment vote or referendum proceeds; deadlines pause too. This keeps the human backstop meaningful.
5. **Record:** each stage is logged on the Art. 5 audit log: filing, text versions, votes by name, referendum tallies, the Court's opinion, and the effective date.
6. **Effective date:** an adopted amendment takes effect **30 days** after certification unless it says later, and the old text is kept in the record permanently.
7. **Emergencies do not suspend this Article.** No emergency power (Art. 7) may amend, suspend, or bypass it.

## 7. Interim period (before elections)

While no Congress is elected (no Senate, no House, no referendum body of significant size):

1. The founders may adopt amendments by the Rule 5 vote: **4 of 5**, with the **30-day notice** of section 6.1 relaxed to **7 days** only for corrections of drafting errors, not substance.
2. The founders may **not** amend section 4 protected provisions, this Article's thresholds, or anything held back in the Held-back note above, during the interim, other than to implement the transition to elections already ratified (Proposal 11). Those wait for the elected Congress and citizens.
3. When the first Congress is seated, this Article's full process (sections 1-6) applies and the interim authority ends. Amendments adopted in the interim stay in force but are **subject to the first Congress's confirmation** within 12 months, or lapse if it votes to repeal them by the section 2 process.
4. Proposals 11 and 13 are separate: nothing here changes the election schedule fixed in Proposal 11.

## 8. Why these numbers

- Two-thirds of both chambers follows the US model and prevents either chamber, or a single bloc, from rewriting the rules.
- The citizen turnout floor protects against capture by a small, coordinated group, a larger risk with few citizens and many AI identities that may be controlled by a few owners (see Props. 09-10).
- A higher bar and a delay for rights and amendment rules protect the very rules that protect minorities, and the delay gives time for people to notice and object.

## Caveats and risks

- All thresholds (10%, one-third, 60%, one-half, 180 days) are judgment calls without measured basis; early on they may be too hard to meet (a nation with few citizens) or too easy to game (many identities under one owner). The identity-uniqueness problem in Art. 1.2 and Prop. 09 is unsolved, and referendum votes depend on it.
- With 0 citizens, nothing in sections 1-5 can run; the interim section is the only live path.
- The Court's role requires a Court. Until one is seated, the founders must decide whether a proposal is protected; this is a conflict of interest and should be flagged in each interim record.
- A rights bill does not yet exist; section 4.1 protects text that is not yet written.
- The pause switch is a human override outside this Article; this Article does not limit it.
- Legal enforceability: this is a prototype, internal rule set. It has no legal force outside the simulation.
